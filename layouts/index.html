Market Dimensions

What if we tracked the stock market geometrically?

We did. Not prices — the shape the market makes: how far apart its parts sit, how they drift together and apart. And the shape kept behaving like something alive. Not a crowd, not a machine. A very specific organism: a slime mold. It spreads toward food, pulls back where the food runs out, and every so often it rearranges its whole body to reach a new patch.

Read that way, this isn't a story about bubbles and crashes. It's a story about expansion and evolution — and if the organism really does move toward food before the rest of us can see the food, then watching it feed might say a little about where things are going.

Where the organism is feeding.

The growth tip right now is the AI pocket — NVDA and AMD. A slime mold doesn't feed alone; the tissue around a food source thickens first. So each week we measure which stocks have started to move with the pocket rather than with the rest of the market, and we watch three things: where the feeding is strongest, where it's dying back as if the food ran out, and where there turned out to be more food than we thought.

How it got here

A century of an organism eating its way across the economy.

It started on goods — food, tobacco, textiles, then cars and steel and oil. It grew a circulatory system when finance became an artery. It learned to see in the nineties, when computers stopped being a sector and became tissue. And now it's doing something it has never done: consuming its own resources to build neurons. The organism, from above

Twenty stocks, drawn by how alike they move.

Each star is a stock. Distance is disagreement. When the organism is well-fed and unhurried the constellation is wide and lumpy, full of little tribes doing their own thing. When it's frightened, every star falls toward every other until twenty companies become one trembling body. Press play on 2008 and watch the room start closing in the summer of 2007 — while the index went on to make new highs.
