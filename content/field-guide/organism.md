title: "The market as a slime mold"
weight: 1
description: "Why this specific organism, what it does that markets also do, and the four questions it makes you ask instead of the usual two."
---

*Physarum polycephalum* is a single cell you can hold in your hand. It has no brain, no nervous system, and no center; it is one continuous body of protoplasm that pulses, and out of that pulsing comes behavior that looks a great deal like deciding. Put oat flakes on a map and it will find the shortest paths between them. Put it in a maze and it will solve the maze — not by searching, but by extending everywhere at once and then withdrawing from every dead end until only the solution is left. It solves problems by being shaped by them.

We didn't start with the slime mold. We started by drawing the market as a cloud of points — stocks placed by how alike they move — and letting time run. The cloud did four things, over and over, that we had no financial word for, and every one of them is something a slime mold does.

**It extends toward food.** When a real source of return appears, the organism doesn't send one stock toward it; the tissue around the source thickens. On the geometry, that shows up as stocks near the source starting to move together — with each other and with the source — before it shows up as relative return. We call the source a *feeding ground* and the thickening the *frontier*. Right now the feeding ground is AI compute and the thickening is in the supply seam: the foundry, the power and cooling gear, the networking. [More →](/field-guide/feeding-grounds/)

**It dies back where the food runs out.** Tissue that stops being fed thins and retracts. In the market this reads as a name losing its connection to the source while its price sags — not a crash, a rerouting. The century-long version of this is the [history page](/history/): energy at a quarter of the whole body in 1980 and under four percent by 1999, with the mass not destroyed but moved.

**It rearranges its whole body to move.** Most of the time capital sloshes: circulates among sectors with no direction, the way protoplasm streams back and forth inside a resting slime mold. But when the organism decides to relocate, that circulation condenses into a few directed rivers pointed at the new food, and once it has arrived, the rivers dissolve back into slosh. We measure how much of the flow is river versus slosh and call it *vorticity*; a falling reading means a migration is underway. [More →](/field-guide/vorticity/)

**It contracts when threatened.** Startle a slime mold and it pulls its extended body back toward its core. Startle a market and every stock falls toward every other until twenty companies are one trembling object. This is the only one of the four behaviors that finance already has words for — bubbles, crashes, contagion — and it is the one this site spends the least time on, because it is rare, and because when it is happening the organism has stopped feeding and there is nothing to read except fear. We keep an instrument pointed at it ([the Observatory](/observatory/)) and a set of tripwires, and then we go back to watching the feeding.

The lens is only worth what it predicts. So each week the machine that runs this site scores the frontier, names the dieback, and flags the places where there turned out to be more food than we thought — and every expectation we form from it is written into a [public ledger](/ledger/) before the fact and graded after. If the slime mold is a good way of looking, the grades will say so. If it isn't, they'll say that instead.

One caution before you go further. A slime mold is a metaphor here, not a mechanism. Nobody is claiming the market is alive. The claim is narrower and stranger: that a market's *shape* — the pattern of who moves with whom — behaves, under measurement, like an organism looking for food, and that watching the shape tells you things the prices don't say yet. Everything that follows is our attempt to find out whether that's true.
