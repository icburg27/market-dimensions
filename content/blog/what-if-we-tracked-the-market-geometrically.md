It started as a game. What if we knew nothing about the stock market — no finance, no theory, no opinions about the Fed — and looked at it the way a particle physicist or a differential geometer would? Not "is it going up," but "what shape is it, and what is the shape doing?"

So we took a basket of stocks and stopped looking at their prices. We looked instead at how they moved *relative to each other*: every pair's correlation over the last sixty trading days, converted into a distance. Two stocks that move in lockstep sit on top of each other. Two that ignore each other sit far apart. Do that for twenty stocks and you get a cloud of points in a space with no dollars in it. Then you let time run and watch the cloud change shape.

## The first thing we saw

The cloud breathes. In calm markets it's wide and lumpy — banks over here, oil companies over there, drugmakers in their own corner, plenty of room between the tribes. There's a number for how much room, called effective dimension, and in a healthy market it's around nine or ten. When fear arrives the cloud collapses: every point falls toward every other until twenty companies are moving as one object and the dimension reads two.

We replayed 2008 and the collapse started in the spring of 2007 and again in August, more than a year before Lehman, while the index went on making new highs. We replayed 2020 and it collapsed in nineteen trading days with no warning at all, then slowly re-inflated. Same bottom, opposite path — one a disease, one an injury. That was interesting enough to keep going.

## The thing that changed the question

We kept expecting the cloud to behave like physics: particles, forces, equilibrium. It didn't. It behaved like something *alive*. It grew toward things. It pulled back from things. When it was frightened it contracted into a huddle, and when the fright passed it re-extended — slowly, one tribe at a time, never all at once. When it rearranged itself, a broad circulating slosh of capital would condense into a few directed rivers running toward a new place, and then dissolve back into circulation once the move was done.

If you've ever watched a time-lapse of *Physarum polycephalum* — the yellow slime mold that biologists put in mazes — you've seen exactly this. A slime mold has no brain and no center. It's one enormous cell that extends toward food, thickens its tissue around a good source, thins and retracts where the food runs out, and, when it decides to move, reorganizes its whole body into veins pointed at the new patch. Give it a map of Tokyo with oat flakes on the cities and it reconstructs the rail network. It solves problems by *being shaped* by them.

Once we saw the market as that creature, we stopped asking the questions we'd been asking. Bubbles and crashes are what the huddle looks like from outside. The more interesting questions are the slime mold's questions: Where is it feeding right now? Where is the tissue thickening around a food source before the food shows up in anyone's numbers? Where is it dying back as if the food ran out? And the one we like best — where did it turn out there was more food than we thought?

## What that looks like today

The growth tip, right now, is AI compute — the pocket around NVDA and AMD. But the organism doesn't feed with one tendril. The tissue around the pocket thickens first, and you can measure that thickening: which stocks have started to move with the pocket instead of with the rest of the market. When we ran it, the names that lit up weren't the chipmakers. They were the foundry, the cooling and power gear, the networking — the supply seam. And two power producers we'd written off as pruned, because their prices had fallen hard, were quietly still connected to the pocket on the geometry.

So we wrote it down. Fifteen names, a thousand pretend dollars, nine predictions registered in a public ledger with due dates, and a promise to grade ourselves honestly in February. Two weeks in, the two "pruned" names are beating everything we favored. That might be noise. It might also be the whole point — the geometry seeing food the prices hadn't priced. Either way it's on the ledger, and it's the first thing in the [weekly reading](/feeding/).

## Where we're going with it

We think this is less a story about the market being dangerous and more a story about the market *evolving* — a creature that spent a century eating goods, then materials, then energy, then grew an artery, then learned to see, and is now, for the first time, consuming its own resources to build something like neurons. [That history](/history/) is a page of its own. The weekly job is smaller and more fun: find the food. Say where we think it's going. Be wrong in public when we're wrong.
