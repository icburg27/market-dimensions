## What you're looking at

Every band is an industry's share of everything listed on American exchanges, measured year by year since 1926. Widen the band and the organism has moved more of its body into that food; narrow it and it has moved on. Watch the flood run and you're watching a century of feeding decisions, none of which anyone made.

## 1926–1945: it eats what can be dug up and hauled

The young organism is mostly mouth. Its biggest single tissue is *Other* — railroads, mines, construction — a fifth of the whole body in 1926, and nearly all of it is about moving material from one place to another. Manufacturing is another sixth. Energy, oil and coal, a seventh. Consumer goods — food, tobacco, textiles — a tenth, and it's the food you eat. Finance barely exists: less than one percent. Healthcare is smaller still. The creature is eating steel, coal, and wheat, and the vessels that carry them.

When the Depression comes, the shape does something a slime mold does under stress: it pulls back into what it knows is safe. Utilities and telephones swell to a quarter of the body by 1932, not because they grew but because everything else shrank around them. Consumer staples hit their all-time peak share two years later. Fear compresses the organism toward the food it is surest of.

## 1945–1975: a manufacturing body, with a nervous system forming

Manufacturing peaks at 23 percent of the market in 1956 — a body built around the factory. Energy stays a steady eighth. But two things start to form that will matter later. Business equipment — office machines, then computers — climbs from five percent to ten by 1960, the first faint sign of a nervous system. And healthcare, which was barely one percent at the end of the war, reaches seven by 1972. The organism is beginning to spend on maintaining itself and on sensing.

## 1975–1985: the energy fever

The oil shocks make the organism gorge. Energy's share doubles to a peak of 26 percent in 1980 — a quarter of the entire body in one food source, the most concentrated the market has ever been in a single industry until the one we're living through. Then the price collapses, and the tissue dies back almost as fast as it grew. Nineteen years later, in 1999, energy is under four percent. This is the clearest dieback in the whole record, and the thing to notice is that it isn't a crash of the body; it's the body rerouting. The mass didn't vanish. It went somewhere.

## 1980–2007: the organism grows an artery

Where it went, first, was finance. From under three percent in the 1960s, the money industry — banks, brokers, insurance — climbs to 18 percent by 1986 and peaks at 21 percent in 2003. In the organism model this isn't a food source; it's a vessel. An artery is the thing other organisms' meals have to pass through, and for a quarter century the market moved a fifth of its body into being the plumbing for itself. That is a strange and revealing thing for a creature to do, and 2008 is what it looks like when an artery gets sick.

## 1990–2000: it learns to see

Business equipment — by now meaning computers, software, semiconductors — goes from eight percent of the body in 1990 to 27 percent in 1999. In a decade, seeing and computing become more of the organism than making things ever was at manufacturing's peak. The dot-com collapse takes it back to 16 percent by 2002. But watch the band afterward: it never gives up the ground it took. Computing didn't turn out to be a food patch. It turned out to be tissue.

## 2009–2017: platforms and pharmacology

After the financial crisis, finance's share falls from 21 to 14 percent and business equipment quietly reclaims the lead — 22 percent by 2017, on its way to becoming the largest tissue in the body's history. Health holds a tenth. Shops, now meaning the online kind, edge up. Durables — cars and appliances, once a tenth of the body — are down to one percent. The measured record ends here.

## 2017–now: feeding on itself to build neurons

This is the part we can't draw from the same series, so the chart shows it as a labelled endpoint and nothing more: as of September 2026, technology is about 38 percent of the S&P 500 by itself, 43 percent if you count half of communication services. No single industry has ever been that much of the body — not energy in 1980, not computers in 1999.

And what it's spending on is different in kind. Every earlier expansion was the organism moving toward *external* food — material, energy, customers, the vessels between them. This one is the organism consuming its own stored resources — cash flow from the platforms, debt, the output of its own chipmakers and power plants — to build capacity for a kind of computation it has never had. Circular financing, the critics call it: the chipmaker funds the model company that buys the chips. In the organism, that's what a growth front looks like from inside. A slime mold extending a new pseudopod feeds it from the body behind it before the tendril finds anything to eat.

Which leaves the question this whole site exists to watch. A growth front either finds external food — revenue arriving from outside the loop — or it dies back, and the body reroutes the way it did after 1980 and 2000. The number that decides it isn't the size of the tendril. It's the flux across its boundary. That's why the [weekly reading](/feeding/) watches the supply seam and the power producers as closely as it watches the chipmakers: the tissue around a real food source thickens before the food shows up in anyone's earnings.

## About the measurement

The bands are the Fama–French 12-industry portfolios computed from the CRSP universe of US-listed stocks: number of firms times average firm size, at year-end, 1926 through 2017, from a public mirror of Ken French's data library. The 2026 marker is S&P 500 GICS sector weights, which is a narrower universe under a different classification — an honest reference point, not a continuation of the line. Industry definitions are the SIC-based Fama–French ones; "business equipment" is computers, software, and electronic equipment. Nothing on this page is investment advice. It is a way of looking.
